Borrowed Standards, Delegated Review: Complaint Routing and Evidentiary Participation in Central Asia's Infrastructure Transition

Which institution must answer when a green infrastructure project adopts one bank's safeguards but receives finance from several? This paper examines the architecture connecting applicable standards, complaint forums, and access to evidence in Central Asia and Mongolia. It asks both where a complaint is directed and what affected actors can contest after it arrives.

The study reconstructs AIIB's end-2024 and end-2025 public cofinancing registers, retaining project-level source locators and financing amounts, and traces the published record of Kazakhstan's Shokpar wind project through October 2026. The later register contains 26 entries for the five Central Asian republics and Mongolia, with USD5,417.78 million in listed AIIB financing. Twenty-three entries, representing USD4,740.28 million, designate a partner institution's accountability mechanism. These designations identify an institutional route; they do not establish the absence of remedy or the eligibility of any particular complaint.

The analysis yields three findings. First, borrowing safeguards and delegating review are separate institutional operations. Across the 152-entry global register, even the best deterministic lookup based solely on the published applicable-policy label must misclassify at least 14 forum designations. The regional comparison includes EBRD-policy entries that designate either AIIB's Project-affected People's Mechanism or EBRD's Independent Project Accountability Mechanism. Second, all 136 entries matched between the two registers retain their policy and forum labels. The changing regional aggregate therefore reflects added operations rather than revised designations among matched entries. An instrument-level sensitivity check further shows why climate policy financing should not be silently counted as a physical renewable-energy project.

Third, Shokpar demonstrates a bottleneck beyond referral. Its complaint reached AIIB in May 2024 and was forwarded to EBRD's mechanism. The subsequent closure report records that four monitoring reports reached the mechanism and bank management, while requesters received summaries rather than full versions. Problem solving ended without agreement; the retrieved registry places the still-open case at compliance assessment. This is evidence of unequal access within a functioning complaint route, rather than proof that delegation caused ecological harm or that no review occurred.

Reading this trajectory alongside the 2026 PPM rules distinguishes continuing coordination from the additional management-report trigger following a cofinancier's noncompliance finding. The revised rules are not applied retroactively to the 2024 submission. The contribution is a reproducible regional reconstruction and a framework for following accountability through four distinct questions: applicable norms, review mandate, evidentiary participation, and remedial outcome. An open source ledger makes those distinctions inspectable for civil society, financiers, and infrastructure practitioners. The paper addresses how standards travel through hybrid financing arrangements and argues that a just transition requires contestability of project knowledge as well as a named complaint mechanism.
